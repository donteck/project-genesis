## Purpose and Authority

The Assessment & Certification Engine determines how LearningOS gathers evidence of learning, interprets capability, supports improvement, and issues trustworthy credentials. Its purpose is not to reward exposure to content. It is to establish what a learner can credibly understand, apply, create, explain, and demonstrate.

Assessment should serve learning before it serves classification. It should help learners recognize progress, identify gaps, receive useful feedback, improve through revision, and present evidence that others can trust. Certification should summarize demonstrated growth without hiding the evidence, criteria, limits, or authority behind the claim.

This blueprint defines the assessment philosophy, five capability levels, assessment types, AI and human responsibilities, certification model, Learning Passport, and product and engineering requirements for LearningOS.

> "Capability is the true credential."

## 1. Why Assessment Matters

Assessment shapes what learners practice, what educators prioritize, what institutions reward, and what outside audiences trust. Poor assessment can encourage memorization without understanding, completion without capability, anxiety without useful feedback, or credentials without credible evidence.

Meaningful assessment matters because it:

- Makes learning goals and quality expectations visible
- Reveals prior knowledge and an appropriate starting point
- Identifies misconceptions before they become durable
- Guides practice, feedback, and next steps
- Helps learners evaluate their own understanding
- Provides evidence of growth over time
- Tests whether knowledge transfers to realistic contexts
- Supports fair and accountable decisions
- Connects projects to portfolios and credentials
- Creates trust between learners, educators, institutions, and opportunity providers

No single assessment can describe a whole person or establish unlimited capability. Every result should be interpreted in relation to the capability assessed, the evidence gathered, the conditions of performance, the criteria used, and the time at which it occurred.

Assessment should create more learning whenever possible. Even summative assessment should provide understandable outcomes, evidence, and a responsible path forward.

## 2. The Assessment Philosophy

LearningOS assessment is governed by the following principles.

### Capability over Completion

Completion records participation in an activity or sequence. Capability requires evidence that knowledge and skill can be used appropriately.

### Evidence over Assumption

Assessment claims should be connected to observable responses, artifacts, decisions, demonstrations, explanations, or performance—not inferred solely from time spent, engagement, or content access.

### Growth over Judgment Alone

Assessment should identify what the learner can do, what has improved, what remains incomplete, and what action can strengthen capability next.

### Authenticity over Convenience

Efficient assessments are useful, but convenience should not replace the conditions required to observe the intended capability. Complex abilities often require projects, demonstrations, or longitudinal evidence.

### Multiple Evidence over a Single Signal

Important claims should draw from evidence appropriate to their consequence. A combination of knowledge checks, practice, projects, feedback, and demonstration is often stronger than one score.

### Transparent Criteria

Learners should understand what is being assessed, why it matters, how evidence will be judged, which assistance is allowed, and how outcomes can be reviewed or challenged.

### Fair Access without Lowered Integrity

Accessibility, accommodations, language support, and alternative response formats should remove irrelevant barriers while preserving the capability being assessed.

### Human Accountability

AI and automation may support assessment, but consequential decisions require governed authority, review, auditability, and meaningful recourse.

> "Evidence is stronger than completion."

## 3. The Five Assessment Levels

### Level I — Knowledge

Knowledge assessment examines whether the learner can recognize, recall, identify, or accurately state relevant information.

**Evidence may include:** retrieval questions, terminology, factual explanations, classifications, definitions, or identification tasks.

**Limitation:** recall alone does not establish understanding or application.

### Level II — Understanding

Understanding assessment examines whether the learner can explain meaning, make connections, compare ideas, interpret evidence, and identify why a concept works.

**Evidence may include:** explanations in the learner's own words, concept maps, comparisons, worked reasoning, error analysis, or responses to changed examples.

**Limitation:** explanation does not by itself establish performance in authentic conditions.

### Level III — Application

Application assessment examines whether the learner can select and use knowledge or skill to solve a problem in context.

**Evidence may include:** scenarios, simulations, labs, exercises, case analysis, debugging, decisions, or practical tasks.

**Limitation:** structured application may provide more guidance and fewer unknowns than independent creation.

### Level IV — Creation

Creation assessment examines whether the learner can integrate capabilities to design, build, revise, and explain an original or meaningfully adapted artifact.

**Evidence may include:** projects, designs, research, software, lessons, media, business artifacts, experiments, plans, or community initiatives.

**Limitation:** an artifact requires process and authorship evidence to establish what the learner personally demonstrated.

### Level V — Demonstration

Demonstration assessment examines whether the learner can perform, defend, transfer, and sustain capability under defined and credible conditions.

**Evidence may include:** live demonstration, presentation and defense, observed performance, verified project evidence, practical examination, external validation, portfolio review, or repeated application across contexts.

**Limitation:** the claim remains bounded by the demonstrated context, criteria, evidence, and date.

The levels are cumulative in strength but not always delivered as a fixed sequence. An authentic project may reveal knowledge, understanding, application, creation, and demonstration together when evidence is designed and reviewed appropriately.

## 4. The Capability Pyramid

The canonical capability progression is:

```text
Knowledge
  ↓
Understanding
  ↓
Application
  ↓
Creation
  ↓
Demonstration
```

The progression can also be viewed as an evidence pyramid:

```text
                    /\
                   /  \
                  / DEMONSTRATION \
                 /------------------\
                /      CREATION      \
               /----------------------\
              /      APPLICATION       \
             /--------------------------\
            /      UNDERSTANDING         \
           /------------------------------\
          /          KNOWLEDGE              \
         /____________________________________\
```

The base is necessary but not sufficient for higher capability. Knowledge supplies material for understanding. Understanding guides application. Application provides experience for creation. Creation produces artifacts through which capability can be demonstrated and defended.

Higher levels generally require richer evidence, more authentic conditions, stronger provenance, and greater reviewer judgment. They should not be reduced to a larger number of low-level questions.

Assessment design should begin at the capability claim and work backward:

1. What should the learner be able to do?
2. Under which conditions should they be able to do it?
3. What evidence would make the claim credible?
4. Which criteria distinguish levels of quality?
5. Which knowledge, understanding, and application support the demonstration?
6. What feedback and reassessment will help the learner grow?

> "Learning becomes meaningful when it can be applied."

## 5. Assessment Types

LearningOS should support a coherent system of assessment types selected according to purpose.

### Diagnostic Assessment

Establishes prior knowledge, skill, misconceptions, interests, language, accessibility needs, or readiness before a pathway or learning activity.

Diagnostic results should guide placement and support without becoming permanent labels.

### Formative Assessment

Provides low-stakes evidence during learning so the learner and educator can adjust explanation, practice, pace, and support.

Feedback should be timely, specific, actionable, and connected to another attempt.

### Practice Assessment

Uses repeated retrieval, exercises, simulations, or deliberate practice to strengthen and monitor developing capability.

Practice should adapt challenge without concealing the standard the learner must ultimately meet.

### Performance Assessment

Requires the learner to perform a task, make decisions, solve a problem, or demonstrate a process under defined conditions.

Performance evidence should preserve relevant observations, conditions, and evaluator criteria.

### Project Assessment

Evaluates an authentic artifact and the process through which it was researched, planned, created, tested, documented, and reflected upon.

Project assessment should distinguish individual contribution, collaboration, AI assistance, product quality, and learning demonstrated.

### Portfolio Assessment

Reviews a curated body of evidence across time, contexts, projects, feedback, reflection, and verified capability.

Portfolio assessment should value depth and provenance rather than artifact volume.

### Summative Assessment

Determines whether defined outcomes or standards have been met at a meaningful checkpoint.

Consequences, required conditions, scoring, review, appeals, and reassessment should be clear in advance.

### Self-Assessment and Reflection

Develops the learner's ability to interpret criteria, evaluate evidence, recognize uncertainty, and choose an improvement step.

Self-assessment contributes insight but should not be represented as independent verification.

### Peer Assessment

Develops judgment and provides diverse feedback through structured criteria, calibration, attribution, safety, and educator oversight appropriate to context.

Peer feedback should not create ungoverned high-stakes authority over another learner.

The engine may combine types within one journey. Assessment burden should remain proportionate, and repeated measures should serve a learning or trust purpose rather than collect data for its own sake.

## 6. AI Assessment

AI can increase the availability, timeliness, and specificity of formative assessment when its role is transparent and governed.

### Appropriate AI Responsibilities

- Generate practice aligned with a capability and difficulty level
- Ask adaptive follow-up questions
- Provide hints and formative feedback
- Compare structured work with explicit criteria
- Identify possible misconceptions or missing evidence
- Suggest test cases, counterexamples, and revision priorities
- Support rubric drafting and evaluator calibration
- Organize evidence for accountable review
- Detect unusual patterns for review without making unsupported accusations
- Explain results and recommend next practice

### Required AI Boundaries

AI should not:

- Issue a consequential credential solely from unreviewed model judgment
- Treat probabilistic inference as observed fact
- Claim authorship, identity, or integrity violations without reviewable evidence
- Grade protected characteristics, writing accent, style preference, or irrelevant behavior
- Use undisclosed external or cross-context data
- fabricate citations, test execution, reviewer consensus, or evidence
- Change criteria after seeing a learner's identity or result
- conceal allowed assistance rules
- prevent meaningful human review or appeal

### AI Assessment Record

Material AI-supported assessment should retain:

- Assessment and rubric version
- Model, policy, prompt, and tool versions where needed for audit
- Input artifacts and permitted context
- Structured criteria findings
- Sources or evidence spans supporting feedback
- Confidence and known limitations
- Human review state
- Learner-visible explanation appropriate to consequence

AI feedback should be evaluated for correctness, educational value, consistency, bias, accessibility, robustness, and effect on learning. Model quality in general benchmarks does not establish fitness for a particular assessment.

## 7. Human Review

Human review provides accountable judgment, contextual interpretation, relationship, and recourse where automation is insufficient or consequence is significant.

### Human Review Is Required or Strongly Indicated When

- A result determines a high-stakes credential or institutional decision
- Evidence is ambiguous, conflicting, incomplete, or novel
- A learner challenges an automated result
- Identity, authorship, integrity, safety, or misconduct is at issue
- Context, culture, disability, language, or accommodation may affect interpretation
- A project requires expert judgment beyond reliable automated criteria
- An exception or prior-learning claim needs evaluation
- A system detects possible bias, drift, or assessment failure

### Reviewer Responsibilities

Reviewers should:

- Apply the published criteria and current assessment version
- Examine relevant evidence rather than rely only on a generated score
- Distinguish individual contribution from shared and AI-assisted work
- Document material reasoning and uncertainty
- Identify conflicts of interest
- Respect accommodations and privacy
- Provide useful feedback and next steps
- Follow escalation and appeal procedures
- Participate in calibration when multiple reviewers assess equivalent work

### Review Quality

LearningOS should monitor inter-rater reliability, rubric clarity, turnaround time, reversal patterns, learner feedback, accessibility, and outcome distribution. Calibration should improve consistent interpretation without eliminating expert judgment.

Learners should know when a human, AI, automated rule, peer, or combination produced an assessment outcome. Appeals should route to someone with sufficient independence and authority to reconsider it.

## 8. Certification Model

A LearningOS certificate is a governed claim that defined capability was demonstrated under stated conditions. It is not merely a receipt for content completion.

### Certification Requirements

A certificate should identify:

- Learner identity or privacy-preserving holder identifier
- Credential title and issuing authority
- Capability or outcomes certified
- Assessment and rubric versions
- Level within the Capability Pyramid
- Evidence types and verification status
- Issue date and, where relevant, expiration or renewal conditions
- Criteria, prerequisites, and permitted assistance
- Credential status and verification method
- Revocation, correction, appeal, and replacement policies
- Links to portfolio or evidence when consent and policy allow

### Credential Types

- **Participation Record:** confirms attendance or completion without claiming capability
- **Knowledge Badge:** recognizes Level I evidence
- **Understanding Badge:** recognizes Level II evidence
- **Applied Skill Credential:** recognizes Level III evidence
- **Project Credential:** recognizes Level IV creation with provenance
- **Demonstrated Capability Certificate:** recognizes Level V verified performance
- **Stacked Credential:** combines compatible evidence into a broader governed claim

Names and visual hierarchy must not make lower-evidence records appear equivalent to demonstrated capability. The credential should communicate the strength and scope of its claim plainly.

### Certification Lifecycle

```text
Eligibility
  ↓
Evidence Collection
  ↓
Assessment
  ↓
Human Review Where Required
  ↓
Decision
  ↓
Credential Issuance
  ↓
Verification and Sharing
  ↓
Renewal, Update, Expiration, Correction, or Revocation
```

Every certificate should remain verifiable independently of a decorative image. Its underlying record should be durable, secure, privacy-aware, and portable.

> "Every certificate should tell the story of demonstrated growth."

## 9. The Learning Passport

The Learning Passport is the learner-controlled, lifelong record of verified learning, demonstrated capabilities, projects, credentials, and growth across LearningOS contexts.

### Passport Contents

The Passport may include:

- Learner identity and verified identity claims
- Goals and journey milestones selected for inclusion
- Capabilities and level of demonstration
- Assessment evidence and result provenance
- Projects and portfolio artifacts
- Certificates, badges, transcripts, and external credentials
- Feedback, endorsements, and reviewer authority
- Reflection and growth narratives approved by the learner
- Issue, expiration, renewal, correction, and revocation status
- Source institution, organization, and verification links

### Passport Principles

- **Learner controlled:** the learner chooses appropriate presentation and sharing
- **Evidence linked:** claims connect to credible evidence or clear verification status
- **Portable:** records support durable export and interoperable standards where practical
- **Selective:** learners can share relevant claims without exposing the entire record
- **Verifiable:** authorized recipients can confirm issuer, integrity, status, and scope
- **Correctable:** errors and disputes have accountable resolution paths
- **Private by default:** visibility does not expand merely because a record exists
- **Longitudinal:** capability can show growth, renewal, and changed evidence over time
- **Plural:** external learning and evidence may be represented with honest provenance
- **Human readable:** people can understand what each claim means and does not mean

The Passport should not become a universal ranking, permanent behavioral profile, or mechanism for denying opportunity through opaque prediction. It records demonstrated evidence and learner-chosen context; it does not define the limit of a person's potential.

### Relationship to LearningOS Domains

Learn supplies knowledge and practice history. Build supplies projects and artifacts. Prove owns assessments, evidence, and credentials. Grow uses approved Passport information to support goals and opportunities. Community contributes governed mentorship, review, and recognition. Account governs identity, privacy, consent, sharing, and connected services.

## 10. Product & Engineering Implications

### Product Implications

#### Design Backward from Capability

Every assessment should begin with the capability claim, conditions, credible evidence, and criteria before question or interface design begins.

#### Separate Learning Modes Clearly

Practice, formative assessment, restricted assessment, project review, credential decision, and portfolio presentation should communicate different assistance, stakes, timing, review, and evidence rules.

#### Make Evidence Inspectable

Learners and authorized reviewers should be able to inspect artifacts, criteria, feedback, versions, provenance, and the basis of important outcomes.

#### Build Feedback into Reattempts

Feedback should lead to a clear practice, revision, reassessment, appeal, or next-step pathway. Failure should not become a dead end.

#### Design Accommodations around the Construct

Alternative formats, timing, input, assistive technology, and support should remove barriers irrelevant to the capability while preserving what the assessment intends to measure.

#### Communicate Credential Strength

Interfaces should distinguish participation, knowledge, applied skill, creation, and demonstrated capability in names, descriptions, and verification views.

#### Give Learners Passport Control

Sharing, visibility, export, revocation display, evidence disclosure, and audience selection should be understandable and privacy-preserving.

### Engineering Implications

#### Version Assessment Definitions

Assessment, item bank, rubric, capability, policy, allowed assistance, scoring, and credential definitions should be immutable by version once used for consequential evidence.

#### Separate Evidence from Interpretation

Raw responses, artifacts, observations, test outputs, and provenance should remain distinct from scores, rubric judgments, AI findings, and certification decisions.

#### Build an Evidence Graph

Stable relationships should connect learner, capability, assessment, criterion, artifact, project version, reviewer, result, credential, and Passport presentation without duplicating canonical records.

#### Use Governed Workflow State

Draft, scheduled, active, submitted, evaluating, review required, decided, appealed, reassessing, certified, expired, corrected, and revoked states need explicit transitions, authority, and audit history.

#### Secure Assessment Integrity

Apply least privilege, server-side authorization, item protection, rate limits, randomized delivery where appropriate, secure timing, artifact integrity, reviewer separation, and incident response proportional to stakes.

#### Engineer AI as Reviewable Assistance

AI assessment services should return structured criterion findings with provenance, confidence, policy version, and human-review requirements. Model output should never silently mutate final credential state.

#### Support Accessibility Technically

Assessment components, timers, media, editors, diagrams, feedback, and proctoring alternatives must support assistive technology and documented accommodations without exposing private accommodation details unnecessarily.

#### Implement Verifiable Credentials

Credential records need stable identifiers, issuer authority, integrity protection, current status, privacy-aware verification, lifecycle events, and portable representations based on appropriate standards.

#### Preserve Appeals and Corrections

Original evidence and decision history should remain intact when an outcome is appealed or corrected. New decisions should supersede rather than silently overwrite the record.

#### Test Fairness, Reliability, and Recovery

Verification should cover scoring consistency, rubric versions, AI drift, reviewer calibration, accommodations, partial submission, offline or network failure, duplicate events, appeals, credential verification, expiration, revocation, export, and restoration.

#### Observe without Over-Surveilling

Collect the minimum events needed for reliability, integrity, learning improvement, and audit. Avoid invasive behavior capture presented as assessment evidence without necessity, disclosure, and governance.
